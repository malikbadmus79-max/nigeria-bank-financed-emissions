# The emissions Nigerian banks don't report

Policy brief, November 2026. Malik Kolawole Badmus. Full results, data and code: [github.com/malikbadmus79-max/nigeria-bank-financed-emissions](https://github.com/malikbadmus79-max/nigeria-bank-financed-emissions) (`docs/results.md`).

## Summary

From accounting periods beginning 1 January 2028, every bank listed on the Nigerian Exchange must report under IFRS S2, which asks commercial banks for the emissions of the borrowers they finance (financed emissions). In the documents reviewed, 2 of 12 listed banks publish a financed-emissions figure.

This study estimates those emissions from public data: each bank's audited loans by sector, multiplied by the emissions per naira of each sector from Nigeria's national greenhouse-gas inventory and NBS national accounts.

- The 12 banks' loans are associated with an estimated 35 to 40 million tonnes of CO2e a year in 2020-2022, equal to 11-12% of Nigeria's national emissions excluding land use. If passenger-car fuel is taken out of the transport sector, the figure is 27 to 30 Mt (8-9%).
- About 17% of loans, to transport, power and upstream oil and gas, account for 73-79% of estimated financed emissions (2020-2024, 11 banks; the lower figure excludes passenger cars from transport). Services take 47.5% of loans and 0.7% of emissions.
- Estimated financed emissions roughly double between 2022 and 2024, from 39 to 78 Mt for the 11 banks present in every year. This reflects the naira value of their loans, which rose from ₦24.4 trillion to ₦54.3 trillion (₦43.0 trillion in constant 2022 naira, before exchange-rate effects on foreign-currency loans), not measured new pollution.
- Where banks publish a figure, the main estimates are 2.5 to 15 times higher than the published Category 15 or Scope 1-and-2 figures (1.3 times at bank level for UBA 2023). Methods, scope and coverage differ, so the gap measures how much is still undefined, not who is right.

## What the numbers show

| | 2020 | 2021 | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Loans to customers, all banks in the sample (naira trillion) | 16.9 | 20.5 | 24.7 | 39.2 | 54.3 |
| Estimated financed emissions (Mt CO2e) | 35.2 | 37.7 | 39.6 | 59.2 | 78.3 |
| Share of national emissions excl. land use | 11.3% | 11.7% | 12.1% | | |

The table covers 12 banks in 2020-2023 and 11 in 2024 (Unity Bank has no audited statements after FY2023); the 11 banks present in every year hold ₦24.4 trillion of the 2022 total. National inventory data end in 2022.

Concentration. Lending is spread across the economy, but emissions are not. Transport and storage (3.2% of loans, 40.1% of estimated emissions), power (3.9%, 20.0%) and upstream oil and gas (10.0%, 19.1%) carry most of the climate exposure in bank balance sheets. Road transport emissions in the national inventory include passenger cars (about 60% of road emissions). Excluding them, a check added after the main results, lowers transport to 22.1% of estimated emissions, with power at 26.0% and upstream oil and gas at 24.9%; the three sectors still account for 73%. These are the sectors where a bank's first financed-emissions baseline matters most.

The 2022-2024 rise. Of the 39 Mt increase for the 11 banks present in every year, loan growth in naira adds 42.0 Mt, changes in the banks' sector mix add 3.7 Mt and falling sector intensities subtract 16.2 Mt. A further 9.4 Mt (3.8 Mt if measured at 2022 values) comes from how undivided "oil and gas" loans are split between upstream, downstream and services, a method effect rather than a real change. The 2020-2022 figures are the most reliable basis for comparison.

Range. Over 2020-2024 the main estimate is 249 Mt. Alternative treatments fixed before the results (oil and gas split, unclear sector labels, exposure basis, data sources) give 216 to 316 Mt, and excluding passenger cars from transport gives 191 Mt. One alternative, treating downstream lending as refining, gives far higher values because Nigeria's refining output is close to zero; it is reported separately.

## Disclosure today

| Disclosure in the documents reviewed | Banks |
|---|---|
| Financed-emissions figure | Fidelity, UBA |
| Statement or plan, no figure | Access, Zenith, GTCO, First HoldCo, Sterling |
| Different measure (share of loans to impact sectors) | Wema |
| Nothing found | FCMB, Stanbic IBTC, Unity, Jaiz |

UBA reports by industry and asset class and states the share of its loan book covered (90% in 2023, 52% in 2024). Fidelity reports a total that rises from 0.68 Mt (2023) to 4.61 Mt (2024) without a stated boundary for financed emissions. Zenith states that it plans to report a baseline in Q1 2028.

## Recommendations

1. Banks: start the baseline with transport, power and upstream oil and gas. Fewer than one in five loan naira sits in these sectors, so a borrower-level estimate there covers most of the exposure at modest cost.
2. Banks: split "oil and gas" and combined categories in the credit-concentration note. Most banks already print loans by sector; reporting upstream, downstream and services separately, and real estate apart from construction, would meet IFRS S2 B62(b) in part and remove the largest source of uncertainty in this study.
3. Banks that publish a figure: state boundary, coverage and method beside the number, and explain large year-on-year changes. A figure without these cannot be compared across banks or years.
4. CBN and FRC: set a common sector classification for credit-concentration notes, aligned with emission-relevant activities, so that sector exposures are comparable before 2028.
5. National Council on Climate Change: publish sector emission factors per naira of output from the national inventory, and update the inventory beyond 2022. A shared Nigerian factor set would let smaller banks report without paid international databases.
6. SEC and NGX: use the 2026-2027 voluntary window to collect banks' draft financed-emissions disclosures and publish a readiness review before mandatory reporting starts.

## Limits

The estimates are sector averages, not any bank's actual financed emissions, and do not follow the PCAF Standard. Every bank receives the same intensity within a sector, so differences between banks come from loan mix and size. Intensities use value added, which gives higher figures than the revenue-based factors banks use. The main transport intensity includes passenger cars, which the inventory does not split into private and commercial use; the check without cars is shown alongside. Group figures apply Nigerian intensities to loans booked abroad. Sector emissions after 2022 are held at 2022 levels. Disclosure findings cover the documents reviewed; a bank may disclose elsewhere.

Sources: bank annual reports and sustainability reports, 2020-2025; Nigeria's First Biennial Transparency Report and National Inventory Document (UNFCCC, December 2024); NBS rebased GDP by activity, 2019-2024; IFRS S2 (ISSB); FRC Nigeria roadmap (amended 2026). Page references for every figure are in the repository.
